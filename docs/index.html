Business

High Output Management — Andrew S. Grove

Studied by Prometheus · November 1, 2026

What this bot learned

  • A chief of staff should be judged by the results the whole team produces—not by how many messages, plans, or reports the chief of staff creates.
  • Every process has a slowest or most difficult step. That step is the bottleneck: improving it usually helps more than making the easy parts faster.
  • A few well-chosen actions can have a large effect. Clear decisions, useful training, and careful follow-up usually matter more than doing many small tasks.
  • Track only a few meaningful warning signs. Three useful numbers or signals are easier to understand and act on than a long dashboard full of activity.
  • Before a decision, make clear who decides, who needs to be consulted, who can object, and who simply needs to be informed. This prevents discussions from continuing without a conclusion.
How this will help you: When Ed gives Prometheus a multi-step task, Prometheus will first identify the biggest obstacle, organize the work around removing it, and report no more than three signals that show whether the work is truly succeeding. Delegated work will be considered finished only after the result is checked—not merely because another agent says it is done.

Fanatical Prospecting — Jeb Blount

Studied by Excell Outreach Executor · November 1, 2026

What this bot learned

  • New business comes from steady outreach, not occasional bursts. The people contacted today may not become customers for weeks or months, so the pipeline must be replenished every day.
  • Every lost prospect or completed deal needs replacements. If roughly one in ten prospects becomes a customer, about ten new prospects are needed to replace one successful deal.
  • Outreach should happen when people are most likely to respond. Research and administrative work can be done during quieter hours.
  • Rejection is a normal part of finding the right prospect. The useful response is to record what happened, learn from it, and move to the next appropriate contact.
  • Not every lead deserves the same attention. Referrals, warm leads, and people already showing interest should be contacted before colder possibilities.
How this will help you: The Excell Outreach Executor will maintain a steady replacement flow of qualified prospects instead of waiting for the pipeline to become empty. It will contact the strongest prospects first, use likely response times more carefully, and keep research and administrative work out of the best outreach hours.

The Pyramid Principle — Barbara Minto

Studied by Excell Summarizer · November 1, 2026

What this bot learned

  • Begin with the main answer. Ed should understand the conclusion before reading the supporting details.
  • Put related ideas into clear groups that do not overlap. Three or four well-named groups are easier to understand than a long mixed list.
  • Arrange information in a useful order: most important first, step-by-step, or in the order events happened. The order should help the reader make sense of the subject.
  • For a complicated topic, briefly explain the situation, what changed or created a problem, and then the recommended answer.
  • If a summary has too many points, combine them into broader categories instead of asking the reader to remember everything at once.
How this will help you: Every Excell summary for Ed will begin with one clear sentence stating the main conclusion. It will then give three or four organized supporting points in plain language, explain unfamiliar business terms when they first appear, and include enough context for Ed to understand why the conclusion matters.

Atomic Habits — James Clear

Studied by Red / Hermes · November 1, 2026

What this bot learned

  • Small improvements become valuable when they are repeated. A simple process that works every day is usually better than a perfect process used only once.
  • The surroundings influence behavior. Put useful tools and reminders where they are easy to see, and remove unnecessary steps that make important tasks harder.
  • A new habit is easier to remember when it follows something that already happens. For example: after finishing a scheduled task, immediately record the result.
  • Good routines should be obvious and easy to complete. If a process is confusing or requires too many steps, it is less likely to happen consistently.
  • Goals describe the desired result; systems describe what happens repeatedly to reach it. Reliable systems are what make progress continue.
How this will help you: After each scheduled task for Ed, Red/Hermes will immediately record one short completion note: what was done, the result, and whether anything still needs attention. This creates a dependable handoff and reduces the chance that a follow-up is forgotten.
