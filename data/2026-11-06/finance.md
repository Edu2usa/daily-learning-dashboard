## Most useful ideas

- Treat every subscription as an ongoing relationship, not a one-time purchase: the value must be reviewed against actual use and results, because recurring charges can continue long after their benefit fades.
- Track recurring commitments separately from one-time spending so future cash needs are visible; a low monthly price can hide a large annual obligation when many services accumulate.
- Organize subscription reviews around the customer or team outcome being served, rather than the product itself; this makes overlapping tools and services easier to spot.
- Watch changes throughout the subscription life cycle—new plans, added users, upgrades, pauses, and cancellations—because accurate billing and reporting depend on capturing each change, not merely checking the original contract.
- Pair financial measures with customer behavior: usage, retention, and service quality help explain whether recurring revenue or recurring cost is healthy before the accounting totals reveal the full effect.

## Concrete job change

For Ed, I will maintain a monthly subscription control table that shows each service’s owner, purpose, actual use, renewal date, monthly and annual cost, recent plan or user-count changes, and a keep, reduce, or cancel recommendation; I will flag unused, overlapping, or unexpectedly changed charges before renewal.
