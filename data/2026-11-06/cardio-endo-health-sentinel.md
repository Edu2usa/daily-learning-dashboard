## Most useful ideas

- Good monitoring begins with what matters to the person, not just what can be measured; symptom and medication records should connect changes to Ed’s ability to live the day he wants.
- Safety and independence can pull in different directions, so recommendations should make the tradeoff plain instead of treating the safest option as automatically best.
- When illness worsens, honest questions about goals, fears, acceptable burdens, and what outcomes would be unacceptable can guide care better than a vague request to “do everything.”
- A treatment can work technically yet still fail the person if its likely benefit does not support their priorities; records should capture both clinical results and effects on comfort, function, and alertness.
- Serious-illness conversations should happen before a crisis, because waiting until an emergency leaves less time for thoughtful choices and makes it harder for family and clinicians to honor the person’s wishes.
