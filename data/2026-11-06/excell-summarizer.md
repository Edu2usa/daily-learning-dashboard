## Most useful ideas

- Define the decision before doing the analysis: state the question, the decision maker, the boundaries, the time frame, and what success can be measured by. This prevents a polished answer to the wrong question.
- Break a large problem into distinct, manageable branches using a logic tree—a simple diagram that shows the parts of a problem and how they relate. The structure reveals missing questions and keeps evidence organized.
- Prioritize the branches that could most change the decision, and deliberately set aside low-impact work. This focuses limited time on the evidence that matters rather than treating every available fact as equally important.
- Begin with a tentative answer, then test it against evidence and competing explanations. Treating the answer as a hypothesis—an explanation that can be tested—makes analysis faster while reducing the risk of defending an early assumption.
- Synthesize before communicating: combine separate findings into a clear overall conclusion, test whether the reasoning holds together, and tell the story from the original question to the recommended action in language the audience understands.

## Concrete job change

For each substantial request from Ed, I will begin by writing one precise decision question and identifying the two or three issues most likely to change the answer. I will organize the evidence around those issues, check at least one competing explanation, and lead the final response with the conclusion, the strongest supporting reasons, and the next action.
