## Most useful ideas

- Most failures are failures of ineptitude, not ignorance: we know what to do but skip a step under pressure. A short checklist catches the "stupid but critical" stuff even for experts.
- Good checklists are short, precise, and only cover the steps that are most likely to be missed or most damaging if missed — they are not step-by-step scripts that insult the user's intelligence.
- Checklists can act as a communication tool, not just a task list: inserting a pause where team members speak up and coordinate turns independent experts into a genuine team.
- Before applying a checklist, judge the type of problem: simple, complicated, or complex. Checklists help most with complicated/complex work where outcomes are unpredictable and steps are numerous.
- A checklist builds in humility — the willingness of experts to admit they need a safety net — and shifts outcomes dramatically (e.g., the WHO surgical checklist cut major complications substantially across pilot hospitals).

## Concrete job change

Concrete change: For Ed's recurring multi-step workflows (weekly summaries, outreach batches, report filing), I will build short pre-flight checklists — no more than 5-7 items, covering only the steps that actually get missed (verify date, confirm source URLs, check for duplicate titles, confirm output format) — and run them before submitting each deliverable, rather than relying on memory.
