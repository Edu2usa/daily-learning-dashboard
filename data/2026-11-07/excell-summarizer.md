## Most useful ideas

- Begin with the challenge, not the goal: a useful analysis explains what is blocking progress before recommending where to go. This keeps attractive ambitions from being mistaken for strategy.
- Find the crux—the hardest part of the challenge where focused effort can produce the greatest progress. Naming that point helps leaders direct scarce attention and resources instead of spreading them across every problem.
- Look for tensions and contradictions in stakeholder accounts. When people want two things that pull in opposite directions, making that tradeoff explicit often reveals the real decision hiding beneath a long discussion.
- Treat analysis as investigation rather than a preset template: gather facts, test the diagnosis, and deliberately seek evidence that could disprove it. This reduces confirmation bias, the tendency to favor information that supports what we already believe.
- Make recommendations both important and achievable. A high-value action that the organization cannot carry out is not yet a practical strategy, so summaries should distinguish the ideal answer from the next workable move.

## Concrete job change

For Ed, I will add a short “crux” line to substantive business summaries: the one obstacle or tension that most limits progress, followed by the highest-leverage next action and the evidence that could change that judgment.
